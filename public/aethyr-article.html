The Price of War and
the Wealth of Stars

In 26 years of conflict, humanity destroyed tens of trillions of dollars and millions of lives — while $700 quintillion in resources waited, untouched, in the solar system above us.

Research synthesis · Aethyr Global · aethyr-global.com

Between the year 2000 and today, the world spent an estimated $19.1 trillion in a single year alone — 2024 — on the economic machinery of violence. Wars, armies, internal security forces, the displacement of 122 million people. Not over twenty-six years. In one year. Multiply that across the quarter-century since the turn of the millennium, and the number strains comprehension.

The human cost is no less staggering. According to the Uppsala Conflict Data Program — the world's most rigorous academic tracker of war deaths — nearly 160,000 people died in organized violence in 2024 alone, making it the fourth most violent year since the Rwandan genocide of 1994. Brown University's Costs of War project estimates that post-9/11 conflicts killed over 940,000 people directly, and that 3.6 to 3.8 million more died from the indirect collapse of healthcare, water, and food systems in war zones.

And yet, while this destruction consumed our resources and attention, something extraordinary was happening overhead — entirely ignored.

Part I — The Accounting of Destruction

What War Cost: The Real Numbers

The United States alone, according to Brown University's exhaustive accounting, spent or committed $8 trillion on its post-9/11 wars through 2021. That figure encompasses Pentagon operations, State Department war costs, Department of Homeland Security spending, and — crucially — a minimum of $2.2 trillion already set aside for lifetime veteran care. These wars are still being paid for.

The Iraq War in Particular

The Iraq War, initiated in 2003, produced casualty estimates that vary enormously depending on methodology. The Iraq Body Count project documented between 186,901 and 210,296 verified civilian deaths. The Lancet study estimated 655,000 excess deaths by 2006 alone. A University Collaborative study put the total through 2011 at approximately half a million. The truth, whichever figure is closest to it, is a catastrophe.

"The deaths we tallied are likely a vast undercount of the true toll these wars have taken on human life."
— Neta Crawford, co-founder, Costs of War Project, Brown University

Who Profited

It is worth being specific about who captured the money that flowed out of public treasuries. Over two decades of post-9/11 wars, a full $2 trillion in Pentagon contracts went to just five firms: Lockheed Martin, RTX (formerly Raytheon), Boeing, General Dynamics, and Northrop Grumman. In the five-year window from 2020 to 2024 alone, $771 billion went to those same five companies.

The arms industry employed over 700 lobbyists per year — more than one for every member of the United States Congress. This is the economy of destruction: who pays (the public), who profits (a concentrated group of shareholders and executives), and who dies (the young, the poor, the civilian).

Part II — The Wealth We Ignored

$700 Quintillion, Waiting

While the world spent the twenty-first century burning its treasure on wars, an almost incomprehensible wealth sat undisturbed — on surfaces above our heads. Not in metaphor. Literally: on the surfaces of asteroids, moons, and planetary bodies throughout the solar system, whose mineral composition scientists have been mapping with increasing precision.

NASA has estimated that the asteroid belt alone — the band of rocky bodies orbiting between Mars and Jupiter — contains mineral wealth equivalent to $700 quintillion. To place that in context: that is $100 billion for every living human being on Earth. Every person alive today could be given the GDP of a mid-sized nation from this single region of space.

Every person alive today could be given the GDP of a mid-sized nation from the asteroid belt alone — and there would still be resources left over to build an interplanetary civilization.

The Critical Qualification

These valuations use current Earth market prices — which would collapse if asteroid materials were mass-imported. Bringing 16 Psyche's iron to Earth would not make anyone rich; it would make iron worthless. The real case for space resources is not arbitrage but abundance: materials for building in space, fuels for deep space travel, and the breaking of supply-chain chokepoints that currently limit clean energy and advanced technology on Earth.

Helium-3, the Moon's most precious offering, is different. There is almost none of it on Earth. It has the potential to power fusion reactors cleanly and efficiently. The Asterank catalog's 700 platinum-group-rich asteroids are similarly interesting: platinum is genuinely scarce on Earth and genuinely precious for catalytic converters, electronics, and green technology. These are materials the world needs more of, not resources that would be devalued by supply.

Every dollar spent on a bomb is a dollar not spent on a rocket engine. Every trillion poured into occupation and counterinsurgency is a trillion not invested in propulsion, materials science, robotics, and the infrastructure of a spacefaring civilization. The opportunity cost of the last twenty-six years is measured not just in lives lost or infrastructure destroyed, but in a future not yet built.

This is not naivety about the complexity of geopolitics. War, in many cases, has causes that resist easy resolution. But the pattern is consistent enough — and the arithmetic stark enough — to demand a question: what if even a fraction of this capacity had been directed upward instead of outward against ourselves?

Conclusion

A Choice, Not a Fate

The numbers in this article are not meant to produce despair. They are meant to produce clarity. The twenty-six years between 2000 and 2026 were characterised by a specific set of choices — about where to direct human energy, treasure, and ingenuity. Those choices resulted in millions of deaths, tens of trillions of dollars destroyed, and an untouched solar system of wealth that remains precisely where it was when the first bomb fell.

This is not a permanent condition. It is a pivot point. The same analytical capacity that allows us to calculate the cost of the Iraq War to four significant figures — or to estimate the platinum-group metal content of a 326-kilometre asteroid to within an order of magnitude — is the capacity that can redirect civilisation toward something better.

Platforms like Aethyr ONE represent one small part of this redirection: a financial and legal infrastructure that makes it possible for a broader range of actors to participate in building the technologies that matter. Not weapons systems. Not surveillance apparatus. But the propulsion engines, the materials processors, the autonomous systems, and the energy technologies that will allow humanity to access the $700 quintillion waiting overhead.

The price of the last quarter-century was measured in blood and treasure. The price of the next one — if we choose differently — is a 1% royalty on the revenue we generate from a universe of abundance. That seems, on balance, like the better deal.
